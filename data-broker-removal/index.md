## Order of steps

- **Inventory first.** List the emails, phone numbers and old accounts you still control. Search each inbox for sign-up and marketing mail to find forgotten accounts.
- **Delete old accounts while you can still sign in.** Some sites only accept deletion from a signed-in session. Finish those before you close the email address that gets their codes.
- **Find your listings and confirm they are you.** Common names pull up other people. Only opt out of a record that is actually yours.
- **Opt out at the official link, in your own browser.** Use the links below. Captchas often fail in automated or remote browsers, so open them yourself.
- **Click verification emails right away.** Links commonly expire in 24 hours, and some much sooner. One in our testing expired in about 15 minutes.
- **Save the receipt.** Keep the confirmation email or screenshot. Log the result from that message, not from memory. A reply that says denied is easy to misread as done.
- **Recheck later.** Look again after the window the site gives (often 3 days), then every month or two. Listings can come back when new public records arrive.

## Pitfalls

- Never pay a people-search site to remove you. Their opt-outs are free.
- Delete linked accounts before you delete an old email address.
- Where you live matters. Many sites only grant formal privacy rights to residents of states with a comprehensive privacy law. Everyone else gets a courtesy opt-out, if that.
- Most state laws exclude publicly available information, such as government records. That is what many listings are built from, so a legal deletion right may not force a takedown.
- Do not misstate your state of residence on a form.
- If an account-deletion email tells you not to sign back in, don't. Signing in can cancel the deletion on some sites.

### Rights depend on your state

SmartBackgroundChecks, USPhonebook and FastPeopleSearch publish near-identical privacy notices. They say your privacy rights depend on your state of residence and point residents of states with comprehensive privacy laws to a supplemental notice. They take requests through a web form or by phone. They also say their data is publicly available information, which they consider outside state privacy laws, and that they apply opt-outs to it as a courtesy. If you live in a state without a comprehensive law, such as Arizona, expect a formal privacy request to be refused. The opt-out form is still worth trying.

## State law map: who has a legal right to deletion

**19 states** have a comprehensive privacy law in effect with a right to delete. Florida's narrow law adds a deletion right that reaches only very large companies. Oklahoma and Louisiana start January 1, 2027, Alabama May 1, 2027, and Vermont January 1, 2028. California's free [DROP](https://consumer.drop.privacy.ca.gov/) tool sends one deletion request to registered data brokers, for California residents only.

**The catch:** most of these laws exclude publicly available information, such as government records, from the data they cover. People-search listings are largely built from those records, so a deletion right may not force a takedown. Sites often apply opt-outs to that data only as a courtesy.

### Arizona

Arizona has no comprehensive consumer privacy law, so Arizona residents have no general legal right to make a people-search site delete a listing. The Arizona Attorney General's consumer pages cover data-breach notice, not deletion, and SB 1815 (2026) was only introduced. Arizona residents can still use each site's free opt-out form. Many sites honor those as a courtesy. Sites that tie formal rights to state law may refuse.

Arkansas: we found no enacted comprehensive privacy law. Some sites cite “Act 549 of 2023,” but the [official Act 549](https://arkleg.state.ar.us/Acts/FTPDocument?ddBienniumSession=2023/2023R&file=549.pdf&path=%2FACTS%2F2023R%2FPublic%2F) is a firearms bill.
